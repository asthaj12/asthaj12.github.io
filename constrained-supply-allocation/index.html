01 · Context

Problem

Launch demand ran ahead of what the contract manufacturer could deliver, and the rule for who got supply first was never written down.

Symptom

Output about 30% below demand

During launches, contract-manufacturer output ran about 30% below demand. Every launch had to decide who received the constrained units first.

How it was decided

Whoever escalated got supply

Allocation was settled case by case through ad-hoc VP escalations. Outcomes depended on who escalated, not on the value each partner or channel returned.

Root problem
No shared rule. The same shortage could get a different answer depending on who escalated, and no published criteria explained the result.
02 · Decision

Decision

Two ways to allocate scarce output. I picked the one with criteria anyone could read.

✕ Rejected

VP-level, case-by-case escalation

Each shortage went to a VP. The answer followed who escalated, not the value of the partner or channel to the launch.

✓ Chosen

One ranked allocation matrix, published criteria

Each partner and channel gets a score: margin × strategic weight. Output fills in rank order until constrained supply runs out, and the criteria are published so every outcome traces back to a rule.

What I owned
I owned the constrained contract-manufacturer allocation and built the matrix. Program and category partners now see one ranking with the trade-offs written out, instead of a set of separate escalations.
03 · Method

Method: ranked allocation in plain words

Score every partner or channel, fill from the top, and write down the tie and minimum rules before the shortage arrives.

1

Score each partner or channel

Score = margin × strategic weight. Margin is a fraction (0.34 for 34%). Strategic weight is a 1–5 scale, written into the matrix so anyone can see it.

2

Fill in rank order

Start at rank 1 and allocate each partner's day-one demand until constrained supply runs out. The partner where supply runs out is the cut line; everyone below it waits.

3

Write down the tie and minimum rules

Ties: the earlier committed launch date ranks first. Minimums: a launch with a committed date keeps a minimum quantity reserved before the rank fill, so a lower score cannot zero out a live launch. The charts below set minimums to zero so the ranking stays visible.

05 · Transfer

Why it matters for long-lead planning

When equipment or components are constrained, the same rule applies: rank first, allocate from the top, and make the cut line visible before the shortage arrives.

Where it applies

Any constrained input with a launch date behind it

Contract-manufacturer output, long-lead components and equipment, wherever demand is ahead of supply and a date is at stake.

What it replaces

Escalation-driven allocation

Outcomes that follow whoever escalated. A published rank follows the criteria, so the same shortage gets the same answer no matter who raised it.

What it makes explicit

Trade-offs for program and category partners

Who is filled first, who waits, and the rule that decided it, in one view both groups can review and re-run.

Transferable pattern
Rank first, then allocate. Score on margin × strategic weight, publish the rule, fill from the top, and show the cut line early enough for program and category partners to plan around it.
