Program Management Case Study

Planning Decision &
Forecast Governance System

An exception-based planning command center built at Walmart US that connects machine forecasts, planner interventions, replenishment outcomes and actual demand into a single accountable chain — so a planning organization covering 4,593 stores can see which decisions helped, which hurt, and which 50,000 weekly exceptions are worth working first.

Scope
Role & Ownership Boundary
Stating this up front, because the distinction is the whole point of the program.
What I own
  • The governance system — the rules, thresholds and audit trail that determine when a planning decision is reviewed and who answers for it.
  • The exception triage process — how 50,000+ weekly forecast and replenishment exceptions get scored, ranked and routed to a human.
  • The S&OP cycle — the recurring operating rhythm where those exceptions get reviewed, decided and closed out with partner teams.
  • Ongoing forecast diagnostics across roughly 130,000 products, and the reporting layer leadership uses to act on them.
What I do not own
  • The forecast engine. Data Science owns the machine forecast model, its features and its retraining. I do not build or tune it.
  • Accuracy and bias are metrics my work is measured against — not assets I control. My leverage is on the decisions layered on top of the forecast, not the forecast itself.
  • Order execution. Replenishment owns the replenishment system and the buy.
  • Item and category strategy. Merchandising owns assortment, pricing and event calendars.
Why that matters
A planning organization can have a well-built forecast and still miss, because thousands of humans quietly override it every week and nobody measures whether those overrides were right. That gap — not the model — is the problem this program was built to close.
01 · Context
Problem
Food & Grocery demand planning across 4,593 stores in 49 states, moving 5–10 billion units a month.
Symptom
50,000 exceptions a week, no way to rank them

The planning systems generated more than 50,000 forecast and replenishment exceptions every week. They arrived as an undifferentiated list. A low-volume tail item and a high-velocity staple flagged the same way, so the queue was worked in whatever order a planner happened to open it — or not worked at all. Volume alone made the queue unusable, which meant the exceptions that actually moved units were statistically likely to be skipped.

Cost
20+ minutes to diagnose one item

Answering "why is this item wrong?" meant a planner manually pulling the machine forecast, the adjustment history, the replenishment output and actual sales from separate places and reconciling them by hand — 20+ minutes per item. At that unit cost, diagnosis was rationed. Planners defaulted to acting on intuition because investigating properly was more expensive than overriding.

Root problem
No feedback loop on manual overrides

The deeper issue was structural. Planners made manual adjustments to the machine forecast constantly, and nothing in the system ever told anyone whether those adjustments helped or hurt. There was no linkage from an override back to the replenishment outcome it produced or the demand that actually materialized. So an override could be a legitimate expert correction or a costly reflex, and both looked identical in the data.

That has three consequences worth naming, because they are what a program has to fix rather than a dashboard: good planner judgment could not be identified and scaled; bad judgment could not be coached; and the forecast engine's true performance could not be separated from the human layer sitting on top of it. Data Science was being evaluated on outputs that had been silently edited downstream.

02 · Framing
Approach
The ask arrived ambiguous — "forecast accuracy is a problem." Framing it was most of the work.
Reframing the question

The instinct in a forecast-accuracy conversation is to go after the model. I deliberately did not, for two reasons: Data Science owns the engine and is better at it than I am, and — more importantly — the evidence pointed elsewhere. The number the business consumed was not the machine forecast. It was the machine forecast plus thousands of undocumented human edits.

So I reframed the problem from "make the forecast better" to "make the decisions on top of the forecast visible, ranked and accountable." That reframe is what made the problem tractable: it turned an open-ended modeling ask into a governance and operating-process program with a clear owner, a clear queue and a measurable cycle time.

Design
The four-layer connection
One item, one row, four layers joined — the structural decision the whole system rests on.
Layer 1
Machine forecast
What the system predicted, before any human touched it.
Owned by Data Science
Layer 2
Planner adjustments & interventions
Every manual override, with size, direction and author.
Owned by Demand Planning
Layer 3
Replenishment outcome
What the supply chain actually ordered as a result.
Owned by Replenishment
Layer 4
Actual demand
What customers bought — the only unarguable layer.
Ground truth

Each of these four layers already existed. None of them were joined. Connecting them end to end is what converts a debate into an audit: with all four on one row you can state, per item, what was predicted, what a human changed, what the supply chain did about it, and whether the customer agreed. The override stops being an opinion and becomes a decision with a traceable result.

Two framing choices followed directly from the design. First, exception-based by default — with ~130,000 products in scope, no reporting surface that asks a planner to browse can work; the system must decide what deserves attention and defend that choice. Second, impact-weighted, not error-weighted — the largest percentage error in the file is usually a low-volume tail item, so ranking by error magnitude sends planners to the least consequential work. Prioritization had to be scored by business impact.

03 · Execution
What Was Built
A planning command center: one diagnostic surface, one ranked queue, one accountability record.
Architecture
Connected diagnostic layer

A data layer in BigQuery that stitches the four sources into a single item-level record, built with SQL and Python and surfaced to planners and leadership in Power BI. The design goal was one screen, one item, complete story — so a planner never has to reconcile four systems by hand to answer a single question.

Prioritization
Impact-based triage queue

Exceptions are scored and ranked by business impact rather than raw error, then routed as a finite, ordered worklist. This is the piece that made 50,000+ weekly exceptions workable: the queue is explicit about what it is asking a planner to skip, so limited planner hours land on the items that move units.

Governance
Override accountability layer

Manual planner overrides are recorded, attributed and evaluated against the replenishment outcome and actual demand they produced. Overrides stayed permitted — planners often know something the model cannot — but they became visible and answerable rather than invisible and free.

How it runs
  • Weekly triage. 50,000+ forecast and replenishment exceptions are scored and ranked by business impact, and the top of the queue is worked inside the S&OP cycle rather than ad hoc.
  • Standing diagnostics. Forecast diagnostics run continuously across roughly 130,000 products, so pattern-level problems surface as patterns instead of as a scattering of separate planner complaints.
  • Override review. Manual adjustments are checked against outcomes, which is how the program surfaced $7.6M in anomalous replenishment adjustments across 34,952 products — a class of exposure that was previously unmeasurable because nobody could connect an adjustment to its result.
  • Escalation path. Findings that are model behavior go to Data Science as evidence; findings that are process or judgment go back through the planning organization. Same data, two doors, no ambiguity about who acts.

04 · Influence
Cross-Functional Execution
Five organizations, none of them reporting to me. Adoption had to be earned, not assigned.

A governance system is the hardest kind of program to land, because the honest reading of it from the outside is "someone built a tool to grade my work." I had no authority over Replenishment, Merchandising, Data Science, Store Operations or Supply Chain. The only workable path was to make each partner's own problem cheaper to solve first, and let the accountability arrive as a by-product of a tool they already wanted.

Partner What they needed How I earned adoption
Replenishment To know which of their orders were driven by a real signal versus an unexamined manual edit. Gave them the adjustment-to-order trace they never had. The $7.6M in anomalous adjustments was framed as exposure we surfaced together, not as a finding against them — which is why the review became a standing item instead of a one-time argument.
Merchandising Confidence that forecast misses on their categories were being diagnosed, not absorbed. Ranked exceptions by business impact so category conversations opened with the items that actually mattered to their P&L, and closed the loop on what was done about each one.
Data Science Clean evidence of model behavior, unpolluted by downstream human edits. Separating the machine forecast from planner adjustments was the single biggest thing I could give them — it let model performance be evaluated on the model. That made them an ally of the governance layer rather than a skeptic of it, and kept the ownership line clean: they own the engine, I own the decisions on top of it.
Store Operations Fewer whiplash swings in what arrived on the truck. Used store-level reality as the tiebreaker in triage. When a diagnosis and a store's lived experience disagreed, the store won and the rule got revised — which is what bought the process credibility in the field.
Supply Chain Advance warning on demand signals that would stress the network. Routed pattern-level findings into the S&OP cycle early enough to be planned around instead of reacted to.
What worked
Leading with diagnostic value and letting accountability follow. Cutting a planner's per-item diagnosis from 20+ minutes to under 3 bought genuine goodwill, and a planner who trusts the tool will accept being measured by it. Governance introduced as a favor lands; governance introduced as an audit does not.
What I'd do differently
I underestimated how much the framing of the override metric would determine adoption. Early on it read as a scorecard on individuals. Reframing it as "which decisions are working" — team-level pattern first, individual second — should have been the design from day one, not a correction.

06 · Reflection
Limits & What's Next
1
The honest limit: this is a detection system, not a prevention system
Every exception the queue surfaces is a decision that has already happened. The system tells a planner an override was wrong after the replenishment outcome exists, which means the cost has usually already been incurred. Making diagnosis dramatically cheaper compresses the correction loop; it does not remove it. The next real step is moving the governance check upstream — flagging a high-risk override at the moment it is entered, not in the following week's review.
2
Triage rules encode today's judgment, and judgment drifts
The impact-based prioritization logic reflects what the business believed mattered when it was written. Category mix, event calendars and store footprints move, and a static ranking rule quietly decays into the wrong ranking rule. This needs a scheduled re-validation of the scoring logic against realized outcomes — governance for the governance system — otherwise the queue slowly loses the credibility that made planners adopt it.
3
Adoption is the fragile part, not the pipeline
The technical layer is stable. What is fragile is a planning organization's willingness to keep working a queue that measures it. That holds only while the tool is visibly more useful to the planner than it is threatening — so the roadmap has to keep paying planners in saved time, not just pay leadership in visibility. I treat that as an ongoing program obligation, not a launch task.
4
Where I'd take it
Close the loop back into the model handoff. The override record is now a labeled dataset of cases where a human believed the forecast was wrong — and the outcome says whether they were right. Those cases are exactly the signal Data Science needs to prioritize model work, and routing them back cleanly is the highest-leverage next move available. It keeps the ownership line intact: they own the engine, I own the evidence about where it needs attention.
Transferable takeaway
The pattern here generalizes past retail demand planning: when humans intervene in an automated system at scale and no feedback loop exists, the intervention layer becomes the largest unmeasured source of error. Instrumenting that layer — and making it accountable without making it adversarial — is a program-management problem, not a modeling one.
