01 · Problem
Context: Hardware Launch Complexity
Why launching consumer hardware is a program-management problem, not a planning-spreadsheet problem.

I owned global supply planning for a ~$400M consumer electronics portfolio — 20+ product lines and 400+ SKUs — and carried each of them through the full product lifecycle, from new product introduction to end-of-life, on a quarterly cadence. That structure creates a permanent, overlapping problem: at any given moment some SKUs were ramping into first production, some were in steady-state replenishment, and some were being wound down, and all three states competed for the same factory capacity, the same components, and the same air-freight budget.

Manufacturing sat with Asia-based ODM partners and contract manufacturers, which meant every decision was made against long lead times. A component commitment made in one quarter landed as finished goods in the next; a missed material window did not surface as a late purchase order, it surfaced as a slipped launch date. Meanwhile demand was regional — NA, EMEA, and APAC distribution centers each with their own sell-in expectations — while supply was global and singular. The gap between those two views is where launches fail.

The core difficulty was not forecasting. It was that no single function owned the launch date. Factories owned build capacity, ODMs owned design and material availability, sourcing owned supplier commitments, and regional demand teams owned the sell-in plan. Each was individually reasonable and collectively unsynchronized. The planning role had visibility across all of them and authority over none of them.

Constraint 01
Long lead times
Asia-based ODM and contract-manufacturing lead times meant material and capacity commitments had to be made well before demand signals firmed. Late detection was equivalent to no detection.
Constraint 02
Material constraints
Component shortages and production constraints were recurring, not exceptional. Every launch plan needed a live view of which materials could actually be secured on the build date.
Constraint 03
Quarterly lifecycle cadence
NPI, sustain, and EOL activity all ran on quarterly cycles across 20+ product lines, so launch readiness always competed against transition and wind-down work on shared capacity.
Constraint 04
Regional vs. global mismatch
Demand was expressed regionally across NA, EMEA, and APAC distribution centers; supply was a single global pool. Allocation decisions were unavoidable and had to be defensible.
Constraint 05
Shortage vs. excess trade-off
Protecting availability with buffer stock raised excess and obsolete exposure at EOL. Protecting against E&O raised stockout risk at launch. Both failure modes were expensive.
Constraint 06
Portfolio scale
400+ SKUs and a ~$400M portfolio ruled out per-SKU heroics. Anything that worked had to work as a repeatable weekly process, not a one-off escalation.
02 · Approach
How Launch Readiness Was Framed
A lifecycle framework plus a constraint-identification loop, both running on the weekly planning cadence.

I treated launch readiness as a supply-side gate rather than a date on a marketing calendar. A launch was ready when material was secured, factory capacity was committed, and the regional demand plan could actually be filled from the global supply position — not when the schedule said it should be. That reframing made readiness measurable week over week, which in turn made slippage visible early enough to act on rather than early enough only to report.

The mechanism was the weekly MRP/MPS planning run in Kinaxis. Every week the plan was regenerated across the portfolio, and every week the same question was asked at each lifecycle stage: what is the binding constraint, and who has to move to clear it? Kinaxis gave the quantitative supply-demand picture; the program work was converting that picture into a short, owned action list and then closing it before the next run.

Phase 04
EOL
Wind supply down against the remaining demand tail so the last builds clear rather than becoming excess and obsolete inventory, and hand the transition into the successor SKU without a coverage gap.
Gate: demand tail satisfied with minimal E&O residual
Constraint-identification methodology
The weekly loop applied to every lifecycle stage
  1. Regenerate the plan. Weekly MRP/MPS run in Kinaxis across 400+ SKUs, producing a fresh supply-demand position for every item in every lifecycle stage.
  2. Run supply-demand gap analysis. Compare regional demand against the global supply position weekly and isolate where projected supply cannot cover projected requirement, by SKU and by region.
  3. Classify the binding constraint. Separate material shortages from production and capacity constraints — the two require different owners and different remedies, and conflating them wastes the week.
  4. Assign the corrective action to an owner. Material issues went to sourcing and the supplier; build and capacity issues went to the factory and ODM; demand-side conflicts went to the regional planning teams and S&OP forum.
  5. Decide the trade-off explicitly. Where supply could not cover everything, resolve shortage-versus-excess deliberately through safety-stock sizing and supply allocation rather than by default or by escalation volume.
  6. Close the loop before the next run. Carry every open action into the following week's review so nothing was quietly re-planned away, and so recurring constraints became visible as patterns rather than as fresh surprises.
Why it mattered for launches: proactive constraint identification only has value if it lands before the lead time closes. Running the loop weekly meant a material or capacity problem was raised with the factory or sourcing team while there was still schedule left to recover — which is the entire difference between a launch that moves left and one that slips right.
03 · Program Execution
Worked Example: ~$30M Apple Education Program Launch
The flagship launch in the portfolio, and the clearest test of the readiness framework.
Flagship Launch NPI
Apple Education Program — supply readiness and launch execution
NPI · ODM coordination · Material readiness · Regional supply positioning

The Apple Education Program launch was a ~$30M program inside the ~$400M portfolio and the highest-visibility NPI I carried. It had the full set of hardware launch dependencies: an Asia-based ODM building to a fixed window, long-lead components that had to be committed before demand firmed, a factory schedule shared with other product lines in the same quarterly cycle, and regional distribution centers across NA, EMEA, and APAC that all needed stock in position at launch.

My role was to own supply readiness end to end. That meant establishing what the factory could actually build and by when, driving the material picture with sourcing and the ODM until every long-lead component had a confirmed commitment against the build schedule, and then sequencing production output against the regional demand plan so launch stock landed where it was needed rather than merely being produced on time.

The dependency management was the substance of the work. Material readiness gated the build; the build gated regional positioning; regional positioning gated the launch date. Because those dependencies were serial, every week of ambiguity anywhere in the chain cost a week at the end of it. So the program ran on the same weekly loop as the rest of the portfolio, with the constraint list reviewed with factories, ODM partners, and sourcing until it was empty — shortages resolved with suppliers, production constraints resolved with the factory, and the remaining risk converted into an explicit allocation decision rather than left as an open question.

Working the constraints proactively rather than reactively is what pulled the schedule forward. Material and capacity issues were identified and cleared while there was still recovery room inside the lead time, which compounded across the build and positioning phases. The program delivered approximately three weeks ahead of schedule — roughly a 15% acceleration against the original plan.

04 · Coordination
Driving Alignment Without Direct Authority
Factories, ODM partners, sourcing, regional demand teams, and S&OP stakeholders across NA, EMEA, and APAC — none of whom reported to me.

I had no authority over any of the groups whose decisions determined the launch date. Factories and ODM partners set build capability, sourcing controlled supplier commitments, and regional demand teams owned the sell-in plan. What the planning seat did have was the only complete view of the supply-demand position — and that view, published on a predictable weekly cadence, was the lever.

The approach was to make the constraint the subject of the conversation rather than the schedule. Instead of asking a factory to commit to a date, I brought the specific material or capacity gap, its quantified consequence on regional fill, and a proposed corrective action with a named owner. That reframing turned an escalation into a decision, and it worked across time zones and organizational boundaries because the underlying numbers were the same for everyone in the room.

Allocation was where the absence of authority mattered most. When global supply could not satisfy every region, someone had to be told no. Sizing safety stock with stochastic models and applying explicit supply allocation rules meant those decisions rested on stated variability and stated priorities rather than on whoever pushed hardest — which is what made them hold in the S&OP forum rather than being relitigated every week.

06 · Transferable Lessons
What This Demonstrates
Generalizable to hardware program management and capacity / launch planning at portfolio scale.
Lesson 01
Launch dates are supply gates, not calendar entries
Defining readiness as material secured + capacity committed + regional demand fillable made slippage measurable weekly. A launch date that is only a commitment cannot be managed; a launch date backed by gate conditions can.
Lesson 02
Early detection is the only lever on long lead times
With Asia-based ODM lead times, the recovery window closes long before the launch date does. Proactive constraint identification is what pulled ~3 weeks out of a ~$30M program and what removed ~$200K of premium freight.
Lesson 03
Separate material constraints from capacity constraints
They have different owners and different remedies. Classifying the binding constraint before escalating is what turned a weekly plan output into a short, actionable list rather than a generalized alarm.
Lesson 04
Manage NPI and EOL as one system
Launch and wind-down compete for the same capacity, components, and freight. Running the full lifecycle on one quarterly cadence is what let availability improve 40% while E&O cost fell ~$1M annually — outcomes that look opposed if the phases are owned separately.
Lesson 05
Influence comes from owning the shared number
Across factories, ODMs, sourcing, and NA/EMEA/APAC demand teams, the weekly supply-demand position was the one artifact everyone accepted. Publishing it on a predictable cadence produced alignment that no reporting line existed to enforce.
Lesson 06
Make the shortage-versus-excess trade-off explicit
Stochastic safety stock and stated allocation rules turned an implicit judgment call into a decision that could be reviewed, defended, and repeated across 400+ SKUs — which is what made it survive contact with competing regional priorities.
Scope note: this work sits squarely in consumer hardware — supply planning and S&OP across a ~$400M portfolio, NPI-through-EOL lifecycle management on a quarterly cadence, and direct execution with Asia-based ODM partners and contract manufacturers on material readiness, capacity, and launch schedules.
