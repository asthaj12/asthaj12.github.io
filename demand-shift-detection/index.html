Demand Shift Detection:
Sustained vs. Temporary Change

A model switch now has to prove a sustained change in customer behavior before it can reach store orders.

01 · Context

Problem

The forecast was moving store orders even when customers had not changed.

Symptom

About 185K candidate switches per cycle, triggered by noise

The forecasting system re-selected its best-fit model every cycle. About 185K candidate model switches per cycle were triggered by noise in the data, not by real changes in customer behavior.

Cost

Every switch moved store orders

Each switch changed the forecast that drives store orders. Replenishment received volatile swings that the system had created itself, and nothing in the signal said whether a swing reflected real demand or a model reacting to noise.

Root problem

Best-fit selection cannot tell a spike from a shift

Picking the best-fit model each cycle answers one question: which model fits the latest data best? A one-cycle spike and a lasting change in customer behavior both pull that answer around, so the system reacted to both. The question that mattered was different: has customer behavior changed, and will it stay changed?

The forecast engine itself was not mine to change. Data Science owns the models. The gap I could address was the rule that decides when a model change is allowed to reach store orders.

02 · Decision

Decision

A model switch has to prove a sustained change before it reaches orders.

✕ Rejected

Best-fit switching every cycle

Switch to whichever model fits the latest cycle best. It is simple to explain and quick to run, but it reacts to every spike. Every spike that reached the model reached store orders.

✓ Picked

Switch only when a sustained change is proven

A switch has to clear two tests. The new level must persist across a window of cycles, and the move must be larger than that item's normal noise. The flip-back rate, the share of switches reversed within the window, is how we check that the rule is working.

Ownership line
I designed the flip-back rate and the noise-scaled gate. Data Science owns the forecast models. My part was the rule that decides when a model change is allowed to reach store orders.
03 · Method

How the gate separates a shift from noise

Three checks, in plain words. Both charts use synthetic illustrative data, not company data.

1

Persistence window

A candidate shift counts only if the new level holds for N consecutive cycles. A spike that fades inside the window never reaches the switch step.

2

Noise-scaled threshold

The move must exceed k times the historical noise for that item. Noise comes from the item's own past cycle-to-cycle variation, so a volatile item has to move further than a stable one before the change counts.

3

Flip-back rate

The share of switches reversed within N cycles. A high rate means the system is chasing noise. A low rate means switches are sticking, which is the evidence that the gate is working.

04 · Results

Outcome

Stability improved. Forecast accuracy did not move.

Accuracy held flat

The gate removed most of the switching that came from noise, and store orders swung less on model changes that did not last. Forecast accuracy did not pay for it: WMAPE went from 14.2% to 14.1%. The stability was gained at no accuracy cost. Estimated value: about $10M.

Companion check: promotion lift

The same discipline applied to promotions. Promotion lift submissions had run up to 3x realized. One baseline per event type, with a 1.25x gate, brought submitted-to-realized from 2.1x to 1.2x and featured-item bias from +14% to +4%. That avoided about $9M of post-event excess.

The trade-off
A gate that waits for persistence reacts later to a real shift than best-fit switching does. A genuine change is confirmed only after the window closes. I accepted that delay because it removed the false switches and accuracy held. The flip-back rate is the measure that shows whether the gate is still doing its job.
05 · Transfer

Why It Matters for Long-Lead Planning

The gate is a rule for when a signal is allowed to become a commitment.

Step 1
Signal
A change in demand or in the best-fit model, seen in one cycle.
Owner: Forecast engine (Data Science)
Step 2
Persistence and noise test
Does the change hold, and is it larger than normal noise?
Owner: Demand planning
Step 3
Store orders
Replenishment acts on confirmed shifts, not on noise.
Owner: Replenishment
Step 4
Long-lead supply
Commit or cancel equipment only on confirmed demand.
Owner: Supply commitments

Long-lead equipment is where a false signal does the most damage. A store order that reverses next week is cheap to correct. A component commitment placed on noise has to be unwound against a supplier's lead time, and it may not unwind cleanly. Separating confirmed from speculative demand before it reaches orders is the same job at both ends of the chain.

What carries over
Before a demand change becomes a commitment, ask three questions. Has customer behavior changed? Will it hold for a set window? Is the change larger than this item's normal noise? Those are the checks the gate runs on store orders, and they are the checks I would bring to long-lead supply decisions.
